# Why Companies Hire a TMC

How to think about the value a [[Glossary#TMC|TMC]] (Travel Management Company) delivers. Once you understand the value, every customer conversation makes more sense.

## Unmanaged vs. managed travel

**Unmanaged travel** is what you already know: book a flight on Expedia or Google Flights, pick a fare, pay with a credit card, get a confirmation, show up. If something goes wrong (flight cancelled, hotel oversold), you call the airline or hotel directly. It works fine when buying one trip for one person.

A **corporate travel program** is the same activity at a different scale, with different stakes. Picture a 600-person company where:

- 200 employees travel at least once a quarter, half of those trips international.
- The CFO needs to know monthly airfare spend.
- Legal needs to know where every traveler is in case something happens.
- Procurement has negotiated rates with three preferred airlines and four hotel chains that need to actually get used.
- The VP of Sales just got stuck in Frankfurt at 11 PM on a Sunday because his United flight cancelled.

That is what a TMC manages.

## The five jobs a TMC does

Every TMC pitch comes down to these five jobs.

| # | Job | What it actually means | What goes wrong without it |
|---|-----|------------------------|----------------------------|
| 1 | **Aggregate content at negotiated rates** | Plugs into multiple airline distribution pipes ([[Glossary#GDS|GDSs]], [[Glossary#NDC|NDC]], direct connects), hotel networks, and rental car systems. Surfaces the company's negotiated rates alongside published fares, in one shopping experience. | Travelers book retail rates on consumer sites. Negotiated rates never get used. Procurement loses leverage at renewal because there is no proof the rates moved volume. |
| 2 | **Service travelers when something breaks** | When a flight cancels at midnight or a traveler misses a connection, 24/7 advisors with the credentials and supplier relationships rebook fast — often using waivers and policies consumer support cannot access. | The traveler spends two hours on hold. Books a walk-up replacement that costs three times the original fare and is out of policy. The company eats the cost. |
| 3 | **Enforce policy without friction** | The booking tool shows in-policy options first, flags out-of-policy choices, requires justification, and routes to an approver when needed. Policy lives in software, not a 14-page PDF nobody reads. | Travelers book whatever they want and expense it. Reviewers either rubber-stamp everything or pick fights after the trip. Compliance is a guess. |
| 4 | **Capture data for negotiation and visibility** | Every booking flows through one system, with categorization, supplier tags, savings tracking, and policy markers attached. Data feeds reporting tools and the next negotiation cycle. | Travel spend is a line item on a credit card statement. Nobody can answer "how much did we spend with Marriott last year?" without a manual exercise. |
| 5 | **Know where every traveler is — duty of care** | Itineraries flow to a risk-management feed in real time. If something happens (natural disaster, civil unrest, terror event, medical emergency), the company knows who is in the affected area and can reach them. | An earthquake hits Tokyo. Legal asks who from your company is there. Nobody knows. |

### The way to remember it

> A TMC sells **access, service, control, visibility, and safety**. Consumer sites sell access only. That is the entire pitch in one sentence.

## When does a TMC pay for itself?

There is no perfect threshold, but the rough math:

- **~$500K/year in travel spend** — Above this, the savings a TMC drives (negotiated rates, fare optimization, policy enforcement, avoided emergency rebookings) typically exceed the management fees.
- **Below $500K** — The math gets closer; a tech-light, low-fee model often wins.
- **$5M+ in annual spend** — The question is no longer *whether* to have a TMC, but *which one*.

See [[TMC Economics]] for the revenue model behind the fees.

### Non-money triggers toward a managed program

These push a company toward a TMC regardless of spend:

- **International travel.** Multi-leg international itineraries are where consumer sites and self-booking tools fall apart. Visas, complex routings, schedule changes, time-zone-aware servicing — these need humans.
- **Regulated industries.** Government, defense, life sciences, financial services need a clean audit trail of every trip; consumer-site bookings do not produce one.
- **Risk profile.** Sending people to high-risk destinations, executives or journalists on the road, or D&O insurance with travel-safety conditions makes duty of care non-optional.
- **Group bookings.** Conferences, board offsites, customer events. The moment you book ten or more people on the same trip, self-service math gets too painful.
